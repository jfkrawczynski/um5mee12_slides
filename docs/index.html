Intro

Fluid mechanics: the equations are known

\[\begin{align} & \boxed{\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla (\rho \vec{V}) = 0}\\ & \boxed{\rho \left[ \frac{\partial \vec{V}}{\partial t} + (\vec{V} \cdot \nabla ) \vec{V} \right] = -\nabla p + \nabla \sigma + \rho \vec{g}}\\ & \frac{\partial \rho E}{\partial t} + \nabla \left[ (\rho E + p) \vec{V} \right] = \nabla (\sigma \vec{V}) + \nabla \vec{q} + \vec{q}_R + \rho \vec{g} \vec{V} + \rho \omega_E\\ & \frac{\partial \rho Y}{\partial t} + \nabla (\rho Y \vec{V}) = \nabla \left[ \rho D \nabla Y \right] + \rho \omega_Y \end{align}\]

But very few analytical solutions! (Poiseuille flow, Couette flow, potential flow around a cylinder, …)

Few exact solutions and… everything else

So: simulate, or measure

Real flows of practical interest are usually turbulent, three-dimensional, geometrically complex, unsteady — exactly where analytical solutions disappear. For the vast majority of real applications, only two paths remain to get a quantitative answer:

Both are costly and neither fully replaces the other: a turbulence closure model is only as trustworthy as the experimental data used to calibrate and validate it.

Why PIV?

Measuring fluid-flow velocity principles (1/2)

  • To determine the velocity of an object, distance and time need to be measured (either one fixing the other).
  • But, gases and pure liquids do not contain moving objects that reveal the velocity.
  • Thus, many measurement techniques have been developed, determining the flow velocity indirectly by measuring another quantity instead (a proxy), such as:
    • pressure (pressure probes),
    • rotational speed (wind anemometer),
    • heat transfer (hot film, hot wire).

None of these is the velocity itself — each relies on an empirical or theoretical relationship (Bernoulli, Joule heating, a calibration curve…) to get there.

Measuring fluid-flow velocity principles (2/2)

What if, instead of inferring velocity from a proxy quantity, we seed the flow with small objects that faithfully follow it? If these objects are visible : back to a purely kinematic (direct) measurement (distance / time), no physical relationship to invert (what makes a good tracer is a question for later).

This opens to optical techniques, differing in how much of the flow they capture at once:

  • Laser Doppler Anemometry (LDA) — a single point, tracked continuously
  • Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV) — a whole plane, captured at once
  • Particle Tracking Velocimetry (PTV) — a few points, tracked continuously
  • Optical Flow — a whole plane, tracked continuously
  • Tomographic PIV — a whole volume, captured at once
  • …

PIV principles (1/2)

  • Seeding: tracer particles are added to the flow.
  • Illumination: these tracer particles have to be illuminated (in a plane) of the flow at least twice within a short and known time interval.
  • Recording: the light scattered by the tracer particles on a camera.
  • Calibration: to determine the relation between the particle image displacement in the image plane and the tracer particle displacement in the flow.

PIV principles (2/2)

  • Evaluation: The displacement of the particle images between the light pulses has to be determined through evaluation of the PIV recordings.
  • Post-Processing: In order to detect and remove invalid measurements and to extract complex flow quantities of interest, sophisticated post-processing is required.

Physical and technical background

Tracer particles (1/6)

  • In contrast to hot wire or pressure probe, velocity estimation in PIV is based on the direct determination of the 2 fundamental dimensions: length and time.
  • On the other hand, the measurement technique is indirect as it determines the velocity of tracer particles instead of the velocity of the fluid.
  • Therefore, the particle / surrounding fluid interaction has to be examined carefully in order to avoid significant discrepancies between fluid and particle motion.

Tracer particles (2/6)

fluid mechanics properties (1/3)

  • A primary source of error: the influence of the gravitational forces on the velocity of the tracer particles.
  • From Stokes’ drag law, assuming spherical particles in a viscous fluid at a very low Reynolds number: \(\vec{U}_g = d_p^2 \frac{\rho_p - \rho}{18 \mu} \vec{g}\).
  • Therefore, the sedimentation of particles can be avoided if the density of the fluid and particles is identical (neutral buoyancy).
  • This is easily achieved in liquid flows, but not in gas flows. Therefore, the particle diameter must be chosen sufficiently small to minimize the settling velocity of the particles.

Tracer particles (3/6)

fluid mechanics properties (2/3)

  • Particle frequency response: \(\frac{d}{dt}U_p = -18 \frac{\nu}{d_p^2}\frac{U_p - U_f}{\rho_p / \rho_f} \Rightarrow U_p(t) = U_f \left(1-\exp \left( t / \tau_p \right )\right)\) with \(\tau_{\rm p} = d_{\rm p}^2 \frac{\rho_{\rm p}}{18 \mu}\), relaxation time scale of the particle.
  • A convenient measure for the tendency of particles to attain velocity equilibrium with the fluid is given by the Stokes number \[ St = \frac{\tau_{\rm p}}{\tau_{\rm f}}\] where \(\tau_f\) stands for the characteristic time scale of the flow (refers to velocity fluctuations along the particle trajectory).
  • A value of \(St < 10^{-1}\) yields an acceptable flow tracing accuracy.

Objective

Estimate a velocity-field from a couple of grayscale particle images, i.e. find \(\vec{V}(\vec{x},t)\) from \(I(\vec{x},t,t+\Delta t)\), \(I \in [0,255]\).

  • Ideally, a high data density is preferred on the PIV vector fields, particularly when significant spatial flow variations must be resolved or when comparing experimental data with numerical results.
  • This requires a medium concentration of the images of the tracer particles in the PIV recording — a real compromise, not a default:
    • too sparse, and matching pairs of particle images cannot be detected by visual inspection;
    • too dense, and particle images start to overlap, blurring the information they should provide.
  • Hence, statistical approaches were developed.

Optimal matching?

Let’s try to find the best shift algorithmically: shift and calculate the sum of squared differences, the minimum of which should be the best shift.

def match_template(img, template, maxroll=8):
    # img: input image to compare
    # template: image to match against
    # maxroll: max nbr of pxl to shift in each dir. Default value is 8.
    best_dist = np.inf
    best_shift = (-1, -1)
    for y in range(-maxroll,maxroll):
        for x in range(-maxroll,maxroll):
            # calculate Euclidean distance
            dist = np.sqrt(np.sum((img - np.roll(template, (y, x), axis=(0, 1))) ** 2))
            if dist < best_dist:
                best_dist = dist
                best_shift = (y, x)
    return (best_dist, best_shift)
# Let’s check that it works by manually shifting the same image (IA):
match_template(np.roll(a_win, (2, 0), axis=(0, 1)), a_win)
(0.0, (2, 0))

Optimal matching?

Let’s use a different matching principle, based on the cross-correlation:

\[R_{II}(x,y) = \sum_{i=-K}^K \sum_{j=-L}^L I(i,j) I'(i+x,j+y)\]

  • \(I(x,y) =\) Pixel grayscale \((i,j)\) of the 1st image (instant \(t\))
  • \(I'(x,y) =\) Pixel grayscale \((i,j)\) of the 2nd image (instant \(t+\Delta t\))
  • \(2K\), \(2L\) = Dimension of the analysis area (mesh).

This is statistical information. We do not look for particle-to-particle speed!

CC interrogation technic

Direct calculation: very long! : \(2M^2N^2\) operations \(\Rightarrow\) Calculation of the correlation function by FFT: \(2N^2\log_2N\) (if \(N=M\)).

Ordre de grandeur: 1000 \(\times\) faster for a 32 \(\times\) 32 window, 10000 \(\times\) faster for a 128 \(\times\) 128 window.

CC interrogation technic

CC is an efficient computational algorithm to find out the right shift.

CC interrogation technic

We can get the first velocity field by repeating this analysis for all windows.

def vel_field(curr_frame, next_frame, win_size):
    ys = np.arange(0, curr_frame.shape[0], win_size)
    xs = np.arange(0, curr_frame.shape[1], win_size)
    dys = np.zeros((len(ys), len(xs)))
    dxs = np.zeros((len(ys), len(xs)))
    for iy, y in enumerate(ys):
        for ix, x in enumerate(xs):
            int_win = curr_frame[y : y + win_size, x : x + win_size]
            search_win = next_frame[y : y + win_size, x : x + win_size]
            cross_corr = correlate(search_win - search_win.mean(), int_win - int_win.mean(), method="fft")
            dys[iy, ix], dxs[iy, ix] = (
                np.unravel_index(np.argmax(cross_corr), cross_corr.shape)
                - np.array([win_size, win_size])
                + 1
            )
    # locate velocity vectors from the center of each window
    ys = ys + win_size / 2
    xs = xs + win_size / 2
    return xs, ys, dxs, dys

xs, ys, dxs, dys = vel_field(a, b, 32)
norm_drs = np.sqrt(dxs ** 2 + dys ** 2)

CC interrogation technic

  • Sub-pixel interpolation of the correlation peak (Gaussian, parabolic, etc.) over 3, 5 or 7 pxls \(\Rightarrow\) theoretical accuracy 0.01 pxl.

  • A residual bias toward integer values can persist: the Peak-locking effect. It is caused when the correlation peak is too narrow to resolve, i.e. when the particle image size drops below ~2 pixels.
  • Solutions: choice of the interpolation function, iterative PIV, etc.

Spatial resolution (1/6)

requirements in fluid flows (1/2)

  • One of the major difficulties in flow imaging is achieving adequate spatial and temporal resolution. This is particularly the case when flows are turbulent, if it is desired to resolve the smallest scales at which fluctuations occur.
  • Laminar flows pose less stringent requirements. In many laminar shear flows including boundary layers, pipe flows, wakes, jets, and mixing layers, the maximum gradient is the same order of magnitude as the overall gradient, \[\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial y} \right)_{\rm max} \approx \frac{\Delta U}{\delta}\] where \(\Delta U\) is the characteristic velocity difference, \(\delta\) is the local width of the shear flow.

Spatial resolution (2/6)

requirements in fluid flows (2/2)

  • In turbulent flows, the spatial fluctuations range in scale from the largest physical dimension of the flow (the ‘‘outer scales’’) to the scale at which diffusion acts to remove all gradients (the ‘‘inner’’ or dissipation scales where the energy of fluctuations is dissipated).
  • The kinetic energy dissipation scale is the Kolmogorov scale \[\eta \equiv \left( \frac{\nu ^3}{\epsilon} \right)^{1/4}.\]
  • Using scaling arguments, the Kolmogorov scale can also be related to outer scale variables through the relationship, \(\eta \propto Re^{-3/4}\).

Spatial resolution (3/6)

choosing the window size

The window must be small enough to resolve the gradient down to the smallest scales, large enough to contain enough particles. Typical starting sizes: \(32\times32\), \(64\times64\), \(128\times128\) px\(^2\) (pixel size \(0.1\)–\(1\) mm).

A window too large smooths and underestimates the true vorticity peak — the classic bias/noise trade-off.

Spatial resolution (4/6)

overlap: more vectors…

Overlapping interrogation windows (typically 50%, 75%) does not add any new physical information — each window is still the same size. It only increases how densely the vector field is displayed.

Spatial resolution (5/6)

resolving strong gradients

Near a wall, or in strong shear:

Far from the wall the correlation peak is sharp: displacement is nearly uniform across the window.

Near the wall it varies within the window itself, partly decorrelating the signal — the peak becomes broad and noisy.

\(\rightarrow\) the fix is not one window size, but a shrinking, iterated sequence of them — see next slide.

Spatial resolution (6/6)

iterative window refinement

Each pass is a small correction to the previous estimate. The window shrinks and rotates to follow that correction.

Validation procedure

Quality factor (signal-to-noise ratio): how much the correlation peak stands out above the next-highest peak — a vector is rejected below a chosen threshold.

Validation procedure

  • Comparison of vectors with their \(k \equiv [i-N:i+N,j-N:j+N]\) neighbors: suppression/replacement if, e.g., \[V_{ij} > \overline{V_k} \pm K \times {\rm RMS(neighbors)}\]
  • Spatial filtering (vectors interpolation): Post-processing is not an artistic creation! It is necessary to remove the noise as much as possible without removing the signal

From one field to statistics

A single realization gives you one instantaneous field. Turbulent flows need many independent realizations to estimate a reliable mean and fluctuations (turbulence intensity).

The error on the estimated mean shrinks as \[\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{N}}\] with \(N\) the number of independent realizations and \(\sigma\) the fluctuation level.

Doubling \(N\) only buys a \(\sqrt 2\) reduction in noise — diminishing returns. Decide before leaving the acquisition station how many realizations you can afford (capture + transfer + processing time), then check afterwards, from your own data, whether the running mean has actually stabilized.

Time-resolved vs. independent realizations

Independent realizations

  • Many uncorrelated double-frame pairs
  • \(\rightarrow\) mean field, turbulence intensity
  • Timing between pairs: irrelevant, as long as they’re independent
  • How many: statistical convergence (previous slide)

Time-resolved sequence

  • A continuous burst of single frames at a fixed rate
  • \(\rightarrow\) spectral analysis (e.g. vortex-shedding frequency, Strouhal number)
  • Frame rate: usually imposed by the light source (laser) and camera.
  • How many: enough periods \(\times\) enough samples/period, not a \(\sigma/\sqrt N\) question

Summary

PIV pros

  • The most widely applied velocity imaging technique in fluid mechanics.
  • PIV is a very robust and accurate technique, which in its most common implementation enables the imaging of two components of velocity in a cross-section of the flow.
  • PIV measurements are now commonplace and they have been applied in a wide range of gas- and liquid-phase flows, including microfluidics, large-scale wind tunnels, flames, plasmas, and supersonic and hypersonic flows.

PIV challenges

Developping a PIV setup is a seek for a compromise regarding,

Homogeneous, regular tracer density \(\rightarrow\) correlation relevance
Reduced inertia of the seeding particles:
  – Neutrally buoyant
  – As small as possible
High visibility:
  – Good light scattering1
  – Large enough
  – No saturation on the CCD sensor
  – No reflection, background noise,…

\(\Rightarrow\) Ideal particle image size (~3-4 pixels): large enough to avoid the peak-locking, small enough to keep good detectability

Poor vs. good seeding — same laser power:

Time between pulses:
  – In plane displacement \(\Rightarrow\) resolution
  – Out of plane displacement \(\Rightarrow\) lost particles

  1. PIV image intensity and contrast \(\propto\) scattered light power: choosing suitable scattering particles is usually more effective and cost-efficient than increasing laser power.
